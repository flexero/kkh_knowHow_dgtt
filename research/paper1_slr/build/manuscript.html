Digital twins for the design, ramp-up and stepwise introduction of flexible manufacturing systems under technology and data constraints: a systematic review

Draft v0.9 (complete manuscript) – 2026-11-13 – author: Kenzo – co-author(s): [supervisor]

Abstract

Purpose – Many manufacturers in emerging industries operate standalone CNC machines and are now moving toward flexible manufacturing systems (FMS). This review examines how digital twins (DTs) support the design, commissioning, ramp-up and stepwise introduction of FMS when technology, budget and data are constrained. Method – A systematic review following PRISMA 2020 was conducted in OpenAlex for 2015–2026. Of 445 screened records, 204 were eligible for full-text assessment; 42 open-access full texts were assessed and 28 studies included. Studies were coded for architecture and communication, uncertainty representation and updating, life-cycle phase, validation, context and a five-level transition scale from standalone CNC to integrated FMS. Findings – 18 of 28 studies describe digital models with manual data exchange. Input uncertainty is represented in 12 studies, almost always by fixed distributions; only 2 update input parameters automatically from real data, and none uses Bayesian updating with plant data. Predictions are compared with reality in 5 studies, and only one compares design-stage predictions with a newly commissioned system (17 % initial cycle-time error). No study covers ramp-up with data, uses Modbus, or follows a transition from standalone CNC to FMS; evidence from emerging economies stays at current-state models or synthetic data. Originality – The review crosses uncertainty handling, life-cycle phase, technology constraints and context, and proposes a research agenda for design-stage predictions that are frozen, updated sequentially with commissioning and ramp-up data, validated against reality and delivered with low-cost technology. Keywords: digital twin; flexible manufacturing system; ramp-up; uncertainty; Bayesian updating; SME; emerging economies

1. Introduction

Flexible manufacturing systems combine numerically controlled machine tools, automated material handling and a central control system so that a variety of parts can be produced with short changeovers. In industrialised economies FMS have been in use for decades, and current research focuses on their integration with digital twins, industrial communication standards and data-driven control. In many emerging industries, however, the starting point is different: standalone CNC machines are common, while FMS, manufacturing execution systems and machine connectivity are only beginning to be introduced.

Introducing an FMS is a large and largely irreversible investment. Decisions on the number and type of machines, pallet and buffer capacity, transport system and control rules must be taken during design, when key parameters – cycle times, failure and repair behaviour, scrap rates and demand – are uncertain. Errors in these decisions become visible only during commissioning and ramp-up, when they are expensive to correct. Simulation has long been used to support such design decisions, but a design-stage simulation model is usually built once, with point estimates, and is rarely confronted systematically with the data that become available after start-up.

The digital twin concept promises to close this loop. A DT is commonly described as a virtual representation of a physical system that is connected to it by data flows; Kritzinger et al. (2018) distinguish a digital model (manual data exchange in both directions), a digital shadow (automatic flow from the physical to the virtual object) and a digital twin (automatic flow in both directions), and found that the literature consisted mainly of concept papers, with applied case studies mostly at the lower integration levels. Since then, reviews have mapped DT applications, enabling technologies and open challenges in manufacturing (Tao et al., 2019; Lu et al., 2020; Fuller et al., 2020). Perspectives on the use of DTs across the system life cycle argue that design and operational decisions should be integrated and that data from operation should inform later design instances (van Beek et al., 2022). Two lines of work address input uncertainty in simulation-based twins directly. Masciarelli and Matta (2022) use metamodel-assisted bootstrapping to build confidence intervals on the performance of a lab-scale manufacturing line when only few data are available, and to identify the parameters with the largest effect; they note that Bayesian approaches are computationally demanding when many simulation runs are needed. Schumacher et al. (2024) fit probability distributions of processing times and scrap rates to data from a real production system and use live fitting to detect changes over time, as input for combined simulation and scheduling optimisation. Reviews of uncertainty in manufacturing describe probability distributions, Bayesian networks, fuzzy logic and grey system theory for modelling it (Siddiqui et al., 2025). On FMS specifically, Florescu (2024) presents a DT developed from the design, sizing and configuration stages through commissioning and operation of an experimental FMS using commercial simulation software, and Ullah and Younas (2024) report a DT-based controller for an FMS built from custom prototypes retrofitted with low-cost hardware, because an industrial FMS was not accessible in their country (Pakistan). The most recent large review classifies DTs in manufacturing along life-cycle stage, hierarchy level, model characteristics, digital-thread connectivity and deployment (Seipolt et al., 2025).

Two recent studies come closest to the design and ramp-up problem. Van Ginkel et al. (2025) state that research on DTs in the design and ramp-up phases is lacking and explore, through three case studies in SMEs, how DTs can be implemented in smart manufacturing system design, virtual commissioning and IoT connection. Kampa et al. (2026) propose a DT methodology for a planned FMS – integrating value stream mapping, discrete-event simulation, production scheduling and CAD/CAM – for a gear manufacturer modernising from conventional machining to an automated system with CNC machining centres, robots, AGVs and an AS/RS, and use it for scheduling and AGV fleet sizing before implementation.

None of these studies, however, follows design-stage predictions through commissioning and ramp-up of a new system, updating uncertain inputs sequentially as data arrive and measuring whether predictions improve. Masciarelli and Matta quantify uncertainty at one point in time; Schumacher et al. update distributions of a system already in operation; Florescu and Ullah and Younas evaluate DTs on experimental or prototype systems without uncertainty quantification; van Ginkel et al. study how to implement DTs in design and ramp-up, but qualitatively; and Kampa et al. use the DT at design stage with deterministic scenarios, without comparing its predictions with the behaviour of the system after start-up.

Most of this work, however, assumes an advanced industrial setting: connected machines, modern communication standards such as OPC UA, existing MES and experienced system integrators. Companies in emerging industries face additional constraints – limited budgets, legacy or low-cost communication (for example Ethernet with Modbus TCP and HTTP only), offline IT environments, and little historical data. Research on DTs in small and medium-sized enterprises and in developing economies reports adoption barriers and notes that much of the evidence comes from developed countries ([SME SLR, 2025]; Cassius et al., 2026). Work on brownfield and retrofit settings shows that integrating legacy machines still requires substantial manual engineering (Tonejca et al., 2024; Wintercorn et al., 2023).

It is therefore unclear to what extent existing DT research supports a company that moves step by step from standalone CNC machines to an integrated FMS under technology and data constraints, and how far DT predictions made at design stage are validated against reality during ramp-up. This review addresses that gap with four questions:

  • SQ1 Which architectures and communication technologies are used for manufacturing DTs, and which of them work with low-cost or legacy technology?
  • SQ2 How is uncertainty of input parameters represented in manufacturing DTs, and how is it updated with real data?
  • SQ3 How are DTs used in the design, commissioning and ramp-up of FMS and production systems, and how are their predictions validated?
  • SQ4 Which studies address SMEs or emerging economies and the stepwise transition from standalone CNC to FMS?

The contributions of this review are: (i) a structured classification of 28 studies across architecture, uncertainty handling, life-cycle phase and context; (ii) a concept matrix that makes empty combinations – research gaps – visible; (iii) a five-level transition model from standalone CNC to integrated FMS used as an analytical lens; and (iv) a research agenda for uncertainty-aware, low-cost DTs for FMS introduction.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 introduces the concepts used. Section 3 describes the review method. Section 4 reports the results per review question. Section 5 discusses the gaps and the research agenda, and Section 6 concludes.

2. Background and analytical framework

2.1 Digital model, shadow and twin

We adopt the classification of Kritzinger et al. (2018) because it is defined by observable data flows and can be applied consistently during data extraction. ISO 23247 (2021) provides a reference architecture for DTs in manufacturing (observable manufacturing elements, data collection and device control, core, user entities), which we use to classify architectures in SQ1.

2.2 Uncertainty in production system models

Input uncertainty in discrete-event models of production systems concerns, among others, processing times, failure and repair times, scrap and rework rates, and demand. It can be represented by point estimates, by probability distributions propagated by Monte Carlo simulation, or by distributions whose parameters are themselves uncertain and updated with observations. In Bayesian updating, a prior distribution p(θ) for a parameter θ is combined with the likelihood of observed data D to give the posterior p(θ | D) ∝ p(D | θ) p(θ). For example, for a failure rate λ with a Gamma(α, β) prior and n failures observed in T operating hours, the posterior is Gamma(α + n, β + T). We record for each study which of these representations is used and whether updating with real data takes place.

2.3 Life-cycle phases

We distinguish design (including sizing and configuration), commissioning (including virtual commissioning), ramp-up (from first part to planned output) and operation. Ramp-up is of particular interest because it is the phase in which design-stage predictions can first be compared with real system behaviour.

2.4 Transition levels from standalone CNC to integrated FMS

To analyse SQ4 we use five levels: L0 standalone CNC (manual loading, paper-based orders, no machine data); L1 connected CNC (machine states and counters collected, OEE measured); L2 flexible cell (one to three machines with pallet changer or robot, programs called by part number); L3 FMS line (several machines, pallet pool or automated transport, central cell controller and scheduling); L4 integrated FMS (FMS linked to MES and ERP, DT updated from real data and used for decisions). Each included study is assigned the level of the system it describes.

3. Method

3.1 Protocol

The review followed PRISMA 2020 (Page et al., 2021). The protocol – review questions, sources, search strings, eligibility criteria, screening procedure, quality criteria and extraction form – was fixed before the search [and registered / agreed with the co-authors on [date]]. Deviations are reported in Section 3.7.

3.2 Sources and search

OpenAlex, an open bibliographic database covering journals and conference proceedings, was searched on 13 November 2026 (title and abstract field, 2015–2026, articles and reviews). Three strings combined a DT/simulation block, a system block and one of three topic blocks (uncertainty and updating; life-cycle phase; context). The full strings are given in Table 1. Full strings: A = ("digital twin" OR "digital shadow" OR "virtual factory" OR "discrete event simulation"); B = ("flexible manufacturing" OR "manufacturing system" OR "production system" OR "machining line" OR CNC); C1 = (uncertainty OR bayesian OR calibration OR "parameter updating" OR "data assimilation" OR "live fitting"); C2 = ("ramp-up" OR "ramp up" OR commissioning OR "factory design" OR "system design" OR "factory planning"); C3 = (SME OR "small and medium" OR "emerging economies" OR "developing countries" OR "low-cost" OR legacy OR retrofit OR Modbus); Sk = A AND B AND Ck. The search script is provided as supplementary material.

3.3 Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they (i) presented a DT or discrete-event model of a discrete manufacturing system, line, cell or machine group; (ii) addressed at least one review question; and (iii) were peer-reviewed. Studies were excluded if they concerned process industries, buildings or other non-manufacturing domains, a single physical process without system view, or presented a concept without model, architecture or case.

3.4 Screening

After duplicate removal, titles and abstracts were pre-screened with the assistance of a large language model (Claude, Anthropic), which applied the written eligibility criteria record by record and recorded a decision, reason code and short justification for each record. The first author verified [all inclusions and a random 20 % sample of exclusions]; agreement between the first author and the pre-screen was κ = [ ] (Cohen's kappa). A second human reviewer independently screened a random 20 % sample; agreement was κ = [ ]. The use of AI assistance is disclosed in accordance with the journal's policy, and all final decisions were taken by the authors. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and the criteria were clarified [if needed]. Full texts of the remaining records were assessed against the same criteria, and reasons for exclusion were recorded.

3.5 Quality appraisal

Each included study was scored on six criteria (clear aim, reproducible method, real data, comparison with a baseline or reality, discussion of limitations, description of context), each 0, 0.5 or 1. Scores were used to qualify the strength of conclusions, not to exclude studies.

3.6 Data extraction and synthesis

Data were extracted into a predefined form (bibliographic data, country, company size, sector, system type, transition level, Kritzinger level, TRL, life-cycle phase, communication technology, cost information, uncertainty representation, updating method, data type, validation type, accuracy measure, decisions supported, limitations, quality score). Results were synthesised descriptively and narratively per review question, and a concept matrix was built to identify combinations of features not covered by the literature. No meta-analysis was performed because study designs and outcome measures were heterogeneous.

3.7 Deviations from the protocol

(i) Only OpenAlex was searched; the planned Scopus and Web of Science searches and snowballing were not performed. (ii) Full-text assessment was limited to reports available in open access (42 of 204). (iii) Screening and extraction were AI-assisted with author verification (Section 3.4). All three deviations are discussed in Section 5.5.

4.2 SQ1 – Architectures and communication technology

Twelve of the 28 studies (43 %) did not state how the model exchanged data with the physical system. Where a protocol was stated, OPC UA was the most frequent (6 studies), followed by TCP/IP (2), Profinet (2), and single uses of MQTT, Beckhoff ADS, REST/HTTP, CoAP, RS485 and file exchange (XML/Excel). No study used Modbus, although Modbus TCP is widely available on low-cost controllers.

Measured performance of the data link was reported only once: van Ginkel et al. (2025) measured a PLC-to-twin delay of 0.98 ms with the PLC simulated on the same computer and 3.02 ms with a real PLC and the twin on a separate computer, using the open-source MQTT broker Mosquitto and Beckhoff ADS. Five studies used low-cost or open technology explicitly: open-source messaging and dashboards in an SME (van Ginkel et al., 2025), commercial depth cameras with object tracking to retrofit a line without changing its control (Ward et al., 2021), an inertial "smart workpiece" costing about 10 USD (Monek and Fischer, 2023), a self-hosted container platform for SMEs (Ramteke et al., 2026; no results yet reported), and RESTful web services with JSON and CoAP (Longo et al., 2019). Only five studies gave any cost figure, and none reported the total cost of the twin itself. In terms of integration level, 18 studies described digital models, 6 digital shadows and 3 bidirectional twins (one only claimed), with 1 review not classified (Kritzinger et al., 2018); in the "twin" cases the link served virtual commissioning or training rather than model updating.

4.3 SQ2 – Uncertainty representation and updating

Input uncertainty was represented explicitly in 12 studies (43 %): fixed probability distributions in 9 (e.g. fitted uniform distributions tested with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, Purwaningsih et al., 2025; normal task times by worker skill, Egilmez et al., 2024; triangular sales scenarios with Monte Carlo simulation, Bendato et al., 2015), empirical distributions in 1 (Fu et al., 2025) and scenarios in 2. Five studies were deterministic and 11 did not state how variability was treated. Assad et al. (2019) explicitly left stochastic cycle times to future work, and Konstantinov et al. (2023) noted that the virtual engineering tools they compared offer no uncertainty quantification.

Automatic updating of input parameters from real data was found in 2 studies (7 %). Fu et al. (2025) maintain operation-time distributions (histograms / kernel density estimates) from PLC timer data and update them as new samples arrive; deviations trigger line reconfiguration by mixed-integer programming. The disturbances were simulated and the authors list physical validation as future work. Monek and Fischer (2023) extract conveyor speed and motion state for a DES automatically from a low-cost IMU and PLC signals in a laboratory, with 99.7–99.9 % speed accuracy against a video reference. Three further studies recalibrated parameters manually (Bokrantz et al., 2017; Konstantinov et al., 2022; Ramteke et al., 2026). Bokrantz et al. (2017) explain why updating is hard in practice: in six industrial DES cases, machine vendors supplied only mean failure values, short stops below one minute were filtered out of monitoring data, and faulty PLC signals and outdated data hindered the construction and maintenance of input distributions. No study applied Bayesian updating to the input parameters of a production system model with plant data, and none quantified how the uncertainty of predictions decreased as data accumulated.

4.4 SQ3 – Life-cycle phase and validation

The design phase was addressed in 18 studies, commissioning in 8 and operation in 14 (several phases per study). No study collected data during the ramp-up of a new system; van Ginkel et al. (2025) discuss ramp-up as a target phase but evaluate the twin in design and virtual commissioning. Decisions supported at design stage were layout (Molnár et al., 2021; Moennich et al., 2026), buffer sizing and operator allocation (Mahmoodi et al., 2025), AGV or AMR fleet size (Purwaningsih et al., 2025; Mahmoodi et al., 2025), workstation number and task sequence (Cabello Oquena et al., 2026), energy-related KPIs (Assad et al., 2019; Caggiano et al., 2016) and capacity (Bendato et al., 2015).

Predictions were compared with reality in 5 studies (18 %). In four, the model of an existing system was validated against historical data: MAPE 3.8 % for total production time (Kamar et al., 2024), no significant difference in delivered pallets by t-test (Purwaningsih et al., 2025), 0.91 % cumulative throughput error over 50 days (Mahmoodi et al., 2025) and 99.7–99.9 % parameter accuracy (Monek and Fischer, 2023). The improvements these models recommended – for example a 14 % throughput gain or 64 % lower material-handling cost – were not checked after implementation. Only Konstantinov et al. (2022) compared design-stage predictions with a newly commissioned system: simulated cycle times of an e-motor rotor assembly station deviated by 17.04 % from the physical station with the initial model and by 8.72 % after manual recalibration, against a target of 5 %; the largest errors (19–28 %) occurred in manual loading tasks whose timing assumptions changed during build. Nine studies compared only with a simulated or historical baseline, and 14 reported face validity or no validation. A review of CAD–DES integration reaches a consistent conclusion: models are usually validated on one short case, and their accuracy over the system life cycle is hardly measured (Chikwata et al., 2026).

4.5 SQ4 – Context and transition level

Systems were classified at transition level L0 in 3 studies, L1 in 3, L2 in 6, L3 in 8 and L4 in 1; 7 could not be classified (manual assembly or not stated). The emerging-economy studies mainly used DES to analyse an existing, largely manual system: an aluminium casting SME in Malaysia (Kamar et al., 2024), a bottled-water SME in Nigeria (Odedairo and Nwabuokie, 2018), packaging lines in Indonesia (Purwaningsih et al., 2025); two studies used synthetic data (Parameswari et al., 2026; Ramteke et al., 2026), and one reported a computational experiment on a machining cell in Bangladesh (Tuhin et al., 2026). Tuhin et al. (2026) propose a phased path – connectivity, monitoring, twin, AI, closed loop – for an existing PLC/CNC cell, and Purwaningsih et al. (2025) simulate the move from manual pallet handling to AGVs. No study followed a company through the transition from standalone CNC machines to an FMS with data, and none from an emerging economy reached beyond the digital-model level with real data.

5. Discussion

5.1 Main findings

SQ1. Communication is often not reported; where it is, OPC UA dominates. Low-cost options are demonstrated only in laboratories or single SMEs, link performance is measured once, and Modbus – common on low-cost equipment – does not appear. SQ2. Uncertainty is represented in fewer than half of the studies and almost always by fixed distributions. Updating from real data is rare (2 of 28), never Bayesian, and never evaluated in terms of how prediction uncertainty shrinks. SQ3. Twins are used mainly at design stage and for virtual commissioning, but their predictions are seldom confronted with the real system; only one study does so after commissioning, with manual recalibration, for one station. Ramp-up is not covered with data. SQ4. Evidence from SMEs and emerging economies exists but is dominated by current-state DES models of manual systems and by synthetic data; the transition from standalone CNC to FMS is not studied.

These findings are consistent with Kritzinger et al. (2018), who found that the literature concentrated on the lower integration levels: seven years later, 18 of 28 included studies remain digital models with manual data exchange.

5.2 Research gaps

  • G1 – Validation of design-stage predictions against ramp-up reality. Only one study compares design predictions with a commissioned system, and none follows a system through ramp-up. The size of design-stage prediction error – 17 % in the only case reported – and how it evolves are unknown.
  • G2 – Sequential (Bayesian) updating of input parameters through commissioning and ramp-up. Updating exists only during operation (Fu et al., 2025) or for single parameters in a laboratory (Monek and Fischer, 2023). Methods that start from design-stage priors and update them as early, scarce data arrive – the situation of a new system – are missing.
  • G3 – Low-cost, measured architectures. Low-cost data paths are demonstrated qualitatively; latency, data loss and cost are rarely measured, and widely available protocols such as Modbus TCP and plain HTTP are not evaluated.
  • G4 – Decision support for stepwise transition (L0 → L4). No study uses a twin to decide which machines join an FMS, in which order, and when, nor follows such a transition with data.
  • G5 – Evidence from emerging economies beyond the digital-model level. Studies from these contexts exist but stop at current-state models or synthetic data.

5.4 Implications for practice

For companies moving from standalone CNC machines to an FMS, the evidence suggests: (i) build the design-stage model with explicit input distributions instead of point values, because data at design stage are mostly vendor means (Bokrantz et al., 2017); (ii) record and date the design predictions, so that they can be compared with reality after start-up; (iii) plan the data path early – the low-cost options in the literature (open-source messaging, inexpensive sensors, camera tracking) show that useful data can be collected without replacing controllers; (iv) expect manual tasks and late design changes to be the main sources of prediction error (Konstantinov et al., 2022).

5.5 Limitations of this review

First, only one database (OpenAlex) was searched; Scopus and Web of Science were planned in the protocol but not used, so relevant studies may be missing. OpenAlex lacks abstracts for some records (63 of 448), which may have reduced recall of title/abstract searching. Second, only open-access full texts could be retrieved: 162 of 204 eligible reports (79 %) were not assessed, so the included set is biased towards open-access venues, including some lower-tier journals; the gap conclusions should therefore be read as "not found in the open-access literature" and should be re-checked against subscription journals. Third, screening and data extraction were performed with large-language-model assistance and verified by the first author [verification to be completed]; assistance can introduce systematic errors, which we mitigated by written criteria, reason codes for every decision and a correction log. Fourth, the transition levels L0–L4 are a new analytical lens and some classifications required judgement. Fifth, several included studies have low quality scores; we used them for mapping but not for strong conclusions.

6. Conclusion

This review examined how digital twins support the design, commissioning, ramp-up and stepwise introduction of flexible manufacturing systems under technology and data constraints. Of 445 screened records, 28 studies were included and coded for architecture, uncertainty handling, life-cycle phase, validation and context. Most published "digital twins" are still digital models; input uncertainty is usually fixed, updating from real data is rare and never Bayesian, and design-stage predictions are almost never checked against the real system after start-up – the single study that does so reports a 17 % initial error. Low-cost architectures and evidence from emerging economies remain at laboratory or current-state level, and no study follows a company from standalone CNC machines to an FMS. We therefore propose a research agenda centred on design-stage predictions that are frozen, updated sequentially with commissioning and ramp-up data, validated against reality, and delivered with low-cost technology – an agenda that a longitudinal case in a greenfield factory in an emerging economy is well placed to address.
